# Roadmap Anti-Patterns

Roadmaps fail in predictable ways. The patterns below recur across organizations, industries, and product types. Recognizing them early — and naming them precisely — is the first step toward corrective action.

---

## Problem Framing

Most teams know their roadmap "isn't working." The symptoms vary: sales overpromises, developers are terrified, stakeholders fight over features, or the roadmap is so detailed it takes a week to update. Beneath these symptoms lie a small set of structural anti-patterns rooted in a single misunderstanding: the roadmap is being used as a project plan, a commitment ledger, or a feature wish-list rather than as a strategic communication tool.

The book's core diagnosis: traditional roadmaps are "rooted in waterfall thinking" and commit to specific features and delivery dates upfront "rather than communicating strategic value and problem-solving intent." Every anti-pattern below is a variation on that root cause. _source: ch01 praise_

---

## The Anti-Pattern Catalog

Each entry includes the canonical name, description, harm caused, corrective action, and key signals.

---

### **Feature- and Date-Driven Roadmap**

A roadmap that lists specific features with delivery dates, used as a project forecast rather than a strategic communication tool. It focuses on "what we can do" rather than customer problems to solve.

**Why harmful.** It roots planning in waterfall thinking. By the time features ship, they may be solving yesterday's problem. Teams are evaluated on delivery of pre-specified work rather than on customer outcomes.

**Fix.** Shift to a theme-driven roadmap that focuses on value, customer problems, and strategic vision rather than committing to unvalidated features or fixed dates.

**Signals.**
- Roadmap lists specific features with delivery dates
- Roadmap is used as a project forecast rather than a strategic communication tool
- Roadmap focuses on "what we can do" rather than customer problems to solve

_source: ch01 praise, David Cancel and Melissa Perri quotes_

---

### **Feature-by-Feature Wish-List Roadmap**

A roadmap structured as a feature-by-feature wish list with fixed delivery dates stretching far into the future — typically a spreadsheet. It "pleases bosses superficially but terrifies developers, requires constant tedious updating, and produces features that are out-of-date before any code is written." _source: ch02 body_

**Why harmful.** The format forces false precision. Planned deliverables are not met, so the roadmap must be tediously updated every quarter. Features are scoped before market feedback is gathered, so many are obsolete on arrival.

**Fix.** Replace the fixed feature-date spreadsheet with a modern, adaptable roadmap that functions as a strategic prototype and communication tool, able to change as the team learns more.

**Signals.**
- Roadmap is a spreadsheet with release dates extending many quarters into the future
- Roadmap must be tediously updated every quarter because planned deliverables were not met
- Developers are terrified by the roadmap
- Features are scoped before market feedback is gathered

---

### **Output-Focused Roadmap**

A roadmap built around implementing specific features and hitting fixed deadlines, with no regard for whether those features solve the underlying customer problem or deliver actual value. Success is measured by whether features shipped on time, not whether they solved customer problems.

**Why harmful.** "Releases happen on time but don't change business KPIs. Implementing customer requests doesn't increase customer satisfaction. Features meet specs without solving the problem for the customer." _source: ch04 §A Roadmap Should Focus on Delivering Value – Problem_

**Fix.** Organize the roadmap around outcomes and themes of customer value; evaluate success against measurable business KPIs rather than against delivery dates alone.

**Signals.**
- Releases happen on time but don't change business KPIs
- Implementing customer requests doesn't increase customer satisfaction
- Features meet specs without solving the problem for the customer
- "I have no insight into how my team's work affects the bottom line"

---

### **Roadmap as Immutable Commitment**

Treating the roadmap as a locked-in promise to stakeholders, leading to a dilemma: either disappoint by delivering outdated solutions, or disappoint by changing course and appearing to have lied.

**Why harmful.** David Cancel's framing captures it exactly: "Either I'm going to disappoint you by giving you exactly what we thought six months ahead of time was the best solution when it's not, or by changing course and having lied to you." _source: ch03 §David Cancel quote_

**Fix.** Frame the roadmap as a living, evolving direction document that communicates intent and priorities, not fixed commitments, so that course corrections are expected and acceptable.

**Signals.**
- Stakeholders hold the product team accountable to items planned six or more months in advance
- Changing the roadmap is treated as failure or dishonesty
- No disclaimers or confidence indicators on roadmap items

---

### **Roadmap as Project Plan**

Treating the product roadmap as a Gantt chart or feature release schedule with specific dates and commitments, rather than as a strategic communication tool. "Sales demands specific dates on the roadmap. Team looks at the roadmap as if it were a project plan." _source: ch04 §A Roadmap Should Not Be Conflated with a Release Plan_

**Why harmful.** Project plans answer "how and when." Roadmaps answer "why and what." Conflating the two forces premature decisions and buries strategic intent under execution detail.

**Fix.** Restructure the roadmap around themes (high-level customer needs/jobs to be done) and vague date ranges; separate tactical execution details into a dedicated release plan or project plan.

**Signals.**
- Roadmap consists of a list of features and dates
- Release plan looks like a Gantt chart with specific delivery dates
- Sales demands specific dates on the roadmap

---

### **Roadmap Without Strategic Context**

Publishing a roadmap so focused on deliverables that it omits explanation of why the organization is pursuing those things, leaving stakeholders without a shared vision.

**Why harmful.** "Product doesn't get the funding, shelf space, or marketing support it needs." Stakeholders fill the vacuum with "shiny object" syndrome and off-vision suggestions. _source: ch04 §A Roadmap Should Put the Organization's Plans in a Strategic Context_

**Fix.** Tie the roadmap explicitly to a compelling product vision linked to the organization's mission, so every item on the roadmap clearly supports that mission.

**Signals.**
- Lots of questions about the details (features, dates) on the roadmap
- Lots of ideas added that don't fit the vision
- "No one knows what our end goal should be"

---

### **Roadmap as Commitment Document**

Allowing the roadmap to be used as a vehicle for specific feature and date commitments extracted by salespeople, executives, or customers, turning intentions into binding promises.

**Why harmful.** "Salespeople write commitments into contracts to win a deal. Customers extract promises before they will sign." The result is a permanent overpromise-underdeliver cycle. _source: ch04 §A Roadmap Should Embrace Learning – Problem_

**Fix.** Commit to outcomes rather than output. Redirect stakeholder conversations from "will this feature ship by this date?" to "what problem are you trying to solve, and can I commit to solving it the best way I can?"

**Signals.**
- Salespeople write commitments into contracts to win a deal
- Customers extract promises before they will sign
- Executives feel date commitments are the only way to get results
- "We always seem to overpromise and underdeliver"

---

### **Features-and-Dates Roadmap**

A roadmap that contains only feature names and release dates — no product vision, no business objectives, no outcome-oriented themes.

**Why harmful.** Without the five primary components (product vision, business objectives, themes, broad timeframes, disclaimer), the roadmap fails to tell the story of *why* and *how*, reducing it to a delivery checklist.

**Fix.** Add the five primary components so the roadmap tells the story of why and how, not just what and when.

**Signals.**
- Roadmap contains only feature names and release dates
- No stated product vision or business objectives
- Work is organized by output rather than customer outcomes

_source: ch05 opening paragraphs_

---

### **Detail Overload**

Adding too many secondary or complementary components to a roadmap, overwhelming stakeholders and obscuring the core message.

**Why harmful.** When the roadmap includes "full project schedules, resource plans, and financial P&L," stakeholders find it overwhelming or distracting. The strategic signal is lost in operational noise.

**Fix.** Be selective: include only the secondary components relevant to the specific stakeholders who will see the roadmap, and keep complementary information in separate targeted conversations.

**Signals.**
- Roadmap includes full project schedules, resource plans, and financial P&L
- All secondary components included regardless of audience
- Stakeholders find the roadmap overwhelming or distracting

_source: ch05 §Secondary Components Added to the Roadmap_

---

### **Feature-Stuffed Roadmap**

Filling roadmaps with features and solutions rather than customer needs and outcomes. "Many product people are used to this approach, and old habits die hard." _source: ch08 §Themes Are About Outcomes, Not Outputs_

**Why harmful.** Roadmap items named after specific technical deliverables (e.g., "HTML5 redesign," "Twitter integration") describe solutions rather than customer problems. The team becomes unable to answer "why" for items on the roadmap.

**Fix.** Transform each proposed feature/output into a theme (outcome) by asking "Why is this important? What will result from doing it?" Use the output-to-theme translation approach — for example, "HTML5 redesign" → "Make mobile experience as good as desktop."

**Signals.**
- Roadmap items are named after specific technical deliverables
- Roadmap items describe solutions rather than customer problems or needs
- Team is unable to answer "why" for items on the roadmap

---

### **Autopilot Feature Acceptance**

Accepting feature requests and stakeholder demands at face value without asking "Why does this matter?", "What need or problem does it solve?", or "Is there a bigger problem that needs solving?" — especially common on teams with long-running products.

**Why harmful.** The team operates in a rhythm of collecting and prioritizing feedback without questioning the underlying need. User journey maps have not been revisited in a long time. The roadmap drifts from customer reality.

**Fix.** Use the user journey map to provide context for where a request fits in the user experience. Ask: Is it a critical path item, or nice to have? Is the unsolved need impeding the user's journey significantly?

**Signals.**
- Feature requests are accepted without asking "why"
- User journey maps have not been revisited in a long time
- Team operates in a rhythm of collecting and prioritizing feedback without questioning the underlying need

_source: ch08 §Existing Product Needs_

---

### **Orphaned Theme**

A theme on the roadmap that does not map back to any strategic objective, indicating it may be misaligned with the product's goals.

**Why harmful.** Cannot articulate which strategic objective a theme supports. Theme was typically added reactively to a stakeholder request without checking alignment.

**Fix.** Review the theme with the team to determine if it should be on the roadmap at all. It may need to be reframed, reworded, or moved to a later column for reconsideration.

**Signals.**
- Cannot articulate which strategic objective a theme supports
- Theme was added reactively without checking alignment

_source: ch08 §Relating Themes Back to Your Objectives_

---

### **Feature-Driven Roadmap Without Problem Context**

Including specific deliverable-level features on the roadmap without describing the problem to be solved, job to be done, or value to the user — "leaving little room for discovery or experimentation." _source: ch09 §Features and Solutions_

**Why harmful.** Items do not describe the problem to be solved or job to be done. Features are listed at the same level as themes rather than nested beneath them. The team has no room to find a better solution.

**Fix.** Frame roadmap items as customer needs or themes rather than specific deliverables. When solutions must be listed, add them as subthemes beneath problem-framed themes so the "why" is retained.

**Signals.**
- Feature labels leave little room for discovery or experimentation
- Items do not describe the problem to be solved
- Features are listed at the same level as themes rather than nested beneath them

---

### **Overpromising and Underdelivering**

Sharing roadmap items as firm commitments to features and dates, leading to disappointment when the team changes course or cannot deliver exactly what was promised.

**Why harmful.** Sales teams treat roadmap items as contractual commitments. No confidence indicators or caveats exist on roadmap items. Customers feel deceived when priorities shift.

**Fix.** Communicate direction and intent (via themes) rather than specific deliverables. Use confidence percentages and hedging language. Be open and honest about priorities so customers understand the roadmap is a direction, not a contract.

**Signals.**
- Roadmap items stated as definite features with fixed release dates
- Sales team treating roadmap items as contractual commitments to customers
- No confidence indicators or caveats on roadmap items

_source: ch12 §The Risks of Sharing – Overpromising and Underdelivering_

---

### **Osborning (Pre-announcing Future Products)**

Announcing future product plans too early, causing current sales to stall as customers wait for the announced future version, potentially leading to revenue collapse.

**Why harmful.** "Dealers or customers canceling existing orders in anticipation of announced future products." Named after the Osborne Computer Corporation, which pre-announced a successor product and saw its existing product sales collapse.

**Fix.** Be selective about what future plans are shared externally and when. Focus external communications on value delivery rather than specific upcoming product versions. When discussing future capabilities, contextualize current product value.

**Signals.**
- Publicly announcing specific future product models or versions before they are ready
- External roadmap showing near-term products that will render the current product obsolete
- Dealers or customers canceling existing orders in anticipation of announced future products

_source: ch12 §The Risks of Sharing – The Osborne Effect_

---

### **Maintaining Entirely Separate Roadmaps per Audience**

Creating completely different roadmaps for each stakeholder group rather than using a modular approach, defeating efforts to align the organization around a common cause.

**Why harmful.** Different stakeholder groups end up with roadmaps that contradict each other. No shared vision or themes exist across audience-specific versions. Roadmap updates must be made independently in multiple documents.

**Fix.** Build a common foundation of vision, strategy, and themes shared across all audiences. Add audience-specific slides or sections on top of this foundation rather than creating entirely separate roadmaps.

**Signals.**
- Different stakeholder groups have roadmaps that contradict each other
- No shared vision or themes across audience-specific roadmap versions
- Roadmap updates must be made independently in multiple documents

_source: ch12 §Multiple Roadmaps? Not So Fast!_

---

### **Gantt Chart or Agile Tracker as the Roadmap**

Presenting development artifacts like Gantt charts, burndowns, burnups, or velocity trackers as the product roadmap, which are "too operational and do not convey product direction or value." _source: ch12 §Complementary Information_

**Why harmful.** No product vision or themes are present. The document answers "what are we building this sprint?" not "where are we going and why?"

**Fix.** Keep project management tools separate from the roadmap. Work iteratively between the roadmap and the project/release plan to check assumptions and set realistic dates, but present the roadmap at the strategic level.

**Signals.**
- Roadmap consists primarily of Gantt chart lines and connectors
- Roadmap artifact is a sprint board or agile velocity tracker
- No product vision or themes present in the roadmap document

---

### **Top-Down Feature Commitment Roadmap**

Executives or founders presenting a roadmap with specific features and dates as directives — "Here's what we're going to build this year" — creating missed expectations when the team cannot deliver as planned.

**Why harmful.** Roadmap is presented as a directive rather than a collaborative plan. Customers receive conflicting feature expectations. Team consistently fails to meet roadmap deadlines because the roadmap was set without their input.

**Fix.** Use theme-based roadmaps that relate to business objectives rather than feature lists. Involve the team in shaping the roadmap. Separate internal and external roadmap artifacts. Include qualifiers and disclaimers in external presentations.

**Signals.**
- Features listed with fixed delivery dates set without team input
- Customers receiving conflicting feature expectations
- Team consistently unable to meet roadmap deadlines

_source: ch12 §Case Study: Chef.io's Roadmap Presentation_

---

### **Operating Sprint to Sprint (No Roadmap)**

A team that has given up on roadmaps entirely and operates purely sprint to sprint or customer demand to customer demand, with no strategic context or prioritization framework.

**Why harmful.** No roadmap exists. Work is driven entirely by incoming customer requests. No product vision or business objectives are articulated. The team cannot rally around a common strategic direction.

**Fix.** Assess your Roadmap Health, determine whether a course correction or full relaunch applies, and re-establish a roadmapping process that provides strategic context and rallies the organization around a single set of priorities.

**Signals.**
- No roadmap exists
- Work is driven entirely by incoming customer requests
- No product vision or business objectives are articulated

_source: ch14 opening paragraphs_

---

### **Futile Prediction Roadmap**

An organization bogged down in effort to predict the unpredictable — creating doomed wish lists of features and dates rather than embracing learning and strategic flexibility.

**Why harmful.** Teams regularly miss commitments. No time is allocated for validation or learning. The roadmap is a long list of features with dates that are almost immediately wrong.

**Fix.** Shift to a roadmap that focuses on customer needs and business objectives, avoids precise delivery dates, and builds in time to learn whether solutions are working.

**Signals.**
- Roadmap is a long list of features with dates
- Teams regularly miss commitments
- No time allocated for validation or learning

_source: ch14 opening paragraphs_

---

### **Force-Fitting a Cookie-Cutter Template**

Attempting to apply a one-size-fits-all PowerPoint roadmap template or project plan to an organization regardless of its unique product, stakeholders, and strategy.

**Why harmful.** The roadmap looks identical to a downloaded template. Stakeholders feel the format doesn't reflect their product reality. The template imposes project-plan thinking by design.

**Fix.** Build a roadmap that fits your specific product and organization through an incremental, stakeholder-inclusive process rather than adopting a generic template.

**Signals.**
- Roadmap looks identical to a downloaded template
- Stakeholders feel the format doesn't reflect their product reality
- Template imposes project-plan thinking

_source: ch14 §Step 6. Keep relaunching_

## When to Use / When NOT to Use

**Use this reference when:**
- Diagnosing why your current roadmap is generating friction, confusion, or distrust
- Reviewing a roadmap artifact before sharing it with a new audience
- Coaching a team or stakeholder who is pushing for a roadmap change that may introduce one of these patterns
- Running a Roadmap Health Assessment and need to name what you're seeing

**Do NOT treat this reference as:**
- A comprehensive list of every possible roadmap mistake — the book surfaces these as the most recurring patterns, not an exhaustive taxonomy
- A scoring rubric where one anti-pattern automatically means a failed roadmap — multiple anti-patterns together signal a deeper structural issue requiring a relaunch, not just a patch
- Applicable in the same way to every organization size or product stage — some patterns (e.g., **Osborning**) are primarily a risk for externally-shared roadmaps; others (e.g., **Operating Sprint to Sprint**) signal the absence of a roadmap entirely

## Code Examples

The book does not present code in the programming sense — it presents structural examples of roadmap artifacts. The following illustrates the output-to-theme translation the book recommends to fix the **Feature-Stuffed Roadmap** anti-pattern:

```
BEFORE (output-focused):
  Theme: HTML5 redesign
  Theme: Twitter integration
  Theme: Admin dashboard rebuild

AFTER (outcome-focused):
  Theme: Make mobile experience as good as desktop
  Theme: Ensure users can share content in the channels they already use
  Theme: Ensure administrators can manage accounts without contacting support
```

The book's corrective for the **Features-and-Dates Roadmap** anti-pattern — adding the five primary components — produces a roadmap structure like:

```
Product Vision:    [One sentence describing the end state for the customer]
Business Objectives:
  - Objective 1 (measurable)
  - Objective 2 (measurable)
Themes (Now / Next / Later):
  Now:   Ensure [result] for [stakeholder]
  Next:  Ensure [result] for [stakeholder]
  Later: Ensure [result] for [stakeholder]
Disclaimer: This roadmap represents our current best thinking and will
            evolve as we learn more. It is not a commitment to specific
            features or delivery dates.
```

Note the Now / Next / Later timeframe model — the book's recommended alternative to fixed-date columns, avoiding the **Roadmap as Commitment Document** anti-pattern. _source: ch05_
