# Roadmap Relaunch Process

## Problem Framing

Most product teams don't decide to relaunch their roadmap process — they stumble into it after months of missed commitments, stakeholder frustration, or the quiet realization that the team is operating sprint to sprint with no strategic thread connecting the work. The pain is specific: sales teams are making promises the product team never agreed to, engineers are asking why a feature matters, and the roadmap is either a stale Gantt chart nobody trusts or a wish list of dated deliverables that's already wrong.

The book's answer is not "switch to a better template." It is a scored assessment of your current process, a branch decision between course correction and full relaunch, and then a six-step path that starts small, builds stakeholder alignment incrementally, and acknowledges that every launch is eventually followed by another relaunch.

---

## The Roadmap Health Assessment

Before choosing an intervention, assess the current state. The **Roadmap Health Assessment** is a 14-question checklist scored on a 0–2 scale (maximum 22 points).

Scoring key for each item:
- **0** = entirely/mostly no
- **1** = sort of / maybe / not sure
- **2** = definitely yes

### Add points when…

| Condition | Reference |
|---|---|
| Clear product vision stakeholders can explain | Ch. 4 |
| Measurable business objectives stakeholders are aware of | Ch. 4 |
| Roadmap focuses on customer needs | Ch. 5 |
| All items tied to customer needs or business objectives | Ch. 2 |
| Roadmap updated regularly | Ch. 10 |
| Time allowed to learn before committing to solutions | Ch. 2 |
| Objective and accepted prioritization method | Ch. 7 |
| Established alignment process with stakeholders | Ch. 8 |
| Regularly present roadmap to key stakeholders | Ch. 9 |
| Regularly present roadmap to customers | Ch. 9 |
| Seek and incorporate customer feedback | Ch. 8 |

### Subtract points when…

| Condition | Reference |
|---|---|
| Specific features/solutions/fixes/deliverables on the roadmap | Ch. 6 |
| Precise or best-case dates on the roadmap | Ch. 9 |
| Solutions thoroughly designed before placing a customer need on roadmap | Ch. 5 |
| Project info (resources, milestones, dependencies) embedded in the roadmap | Ch. 9 |

### Score interpretation

| Score | Meaning | Approach |
|---|---|---|
| 18–22 | Great process | Approach A — tweak and enhance |
| 12–17 | Good shape but significant improvements needed | Approach A — improve one part at a time |
| ≤ 11 | Broken or no process | Approach B — full relaunch |

> Involve stakeholders in completing the assessment. Making the problem visible to all parties builds buy-in for change and surfaces differences in how broken people perceive the process to be. _source: ch14 §Step 2_

---

## Approach A: Course Corrections

Approach A applies when you have a basically functional roadmapping process (score ≥ 12). The intervention is incremental:

- Identify the most promising opportunities for improvement in your current process.
- Prioritize them.
- **Focus on improving one part of the process at a time** rather than changing everything at once.

A small, visible success quickly gathers support for further improvements and reduces the risk of overwhelming the organization. _source: ch14 §Approach A_

---

## Approach B: The Full Relaunch

Approach B applies when the score is 11 or lower — when there is no salvageable process. Start from scratch by following the book's steps in order (Chapters 3–10).

The recommended vehicle is a **Roadmap Workshop**: a focused collaborative event, similar to a Design Sprint, that brings together key stakeholders to define product vision, objectives, customer needs, and other roadmap components over a few days, producing an initial roadmap and agreed next steps.

Limit the initial stakeholder circle to the product core (product managers, engineers, designers), then expand gradually. _source: ch14 §Approach B_

## The Six-Step Relaunch Process

Regardless of which approach applies, the same six-step framework governs the relaunch.

### Step 1 — Assess your situation

Complete the Roadmap Health Assessment (see above). Share results with key stakeholders to create a common baseline. Identify which approach to use.

### Step 2 — Get buy-in for change

Seek **alignment**, not necessarily consensus. Stakeholders may not agree on every detail, but if they agree on the necessity for change, they will give leeway on approach.

Use shuttle diplomacy — one-on-one discussions that play out various scenarios — to arrive at a plan incrementally rather than forcing group consensus. _source: ch14 §Gillian's Story of Roadmap Change_

Roadmapping process change cannot be imposed by fiat. Organizational change crosses boundaries and affects people who must actively participate for it to succeed. _source: ch14 §Step 2_

### Step 3 — Train your stakeholders how to contribute

Product roadmapping is not a solo pursuit; every stakeholder has a part to play and an obligation to contribute. Stakeholders often just need to be shown how to contribute.

Key things to communicate:
- What's different about this approach (no feature or date commitments)
- Each person's specific role and obligation in the process
- Why strategic context — the 'why' behind what is proposed — matters more than feature details. Without it, teams make day-to-day decisions that deliver the wrong things. _source: ch14 §Gillian's Story_

### Step 4 — Start small and work incrementally

- **Approach A:** Pick one part of the process to improve. Set a goal achievable in a few weeks (e.g., adopt a prioritization framework).
- **Approach B:** Run the Roadmap Workshop to produce an initial roadmap, then expand stakeholder involvement gradually.

Look for every opportunity to start small and demonstrate early success. Even a small win gathers support and reduces organizational risk. _source: ch14 §Step 4_

### Step 5 — Evaluate your results and align on next steps

Apply the **Discovery Period** concept to internal process changes: after making a change, pause to learn whether the change had the intended effect before layering on more changes.

A **cross-functional product steering committee** should meet every **three to six weeks** to review progress and align on next steps.

- More frequent than every three weeks is seldom productive — results of change haven't had time to be felt.
- Less frequent than every six weeks prevents the team from gelling and shared context is quickly lost. _source: ch14 §Step 5_

At each steering committee meeting, decide whether to stay the course, change course, or try a new direction.

### Step 6 — Keep relaunching

Roadmapping is a continuous process. Every launch is eventually followed by a relaunch. Acknowledge that change is hard and resistance is normal. Continuously evolve the process rather than treating it as a one-time fix. _source: ch14 §Step 6_

## Anti-Patterns to Watch For

**Operating Sprint to Sprint (No Roadmap)** — A team that has given up on roadmaps entirely and operates purely sprint to sprint, driven by incoming customer requests. No product vision or business objectives are articulated. Fix: Run the Roadmap Health Assessment, determine which approach applies, and re-establish a process that provides strategic context. _source: ch14_

**Futile Prediction Roadmap** — An organization bogged down trying to predict the unpredictable: long lists of features with dates, teams regularly missing commitments, no time allocated for validation or learning. Fix: Shift to a roadmap that focuses on customer needs and business objectives, avoids precise delivery dates, and builds in time to learn whether solutions are working. _source: ch14_

**Force-Fitting a Cookie-Cutter Template** — Applying a one-size-fits-all PowerPoint template regardless of the organization's unique product, stakeholders, and strategy. A roadmap must fit your specific product and organization; any attempt to force-fit a template or project plan will fail. Fix: Build the roadmap through an incremental, stakeholder-inclusive process. _source: ch14 §Step 6_

See also [roadmap-anti-patterns.md](../anti-patterns/roadmap-anti-patterns.md) for the full catalogue.

## When to Use / When NOT to Use

**Use this process when:**
- Your team is operating sprint to sprint with no strategic roadmap.
- Stakeholders have lost trust in the existing roadmap.
- The roadmap is a feature-and-date list that is regularly wrong.
- Your Roadmap Health Assessment score is 11 or lower (Approach B) or 12–17 (Approach A, significant improvements).
- You are inheriting a product function and want to establish or reset the roadmapping process.

**Do NOT use Approach B when:**
- Your score is 12 or higher — you have a salvageable process and a full relaunch will create unnecessary disruption.
- You need only to introduce a better prioritization method, update the timeframe model, or improve stakeholder communication — these are Approach A course corrections.

**Do NOT skip the assessment:**
- Before proposing any solution for organizational change, you must define the problem by assessing your current process. Skipping assessment leads to applying the wrong type of intervention — either unnecessary overhaul or insufficient course correction. _source: ch14 §Step 1_

## Code Examples

The Roadmap Health Assessment scoring logic expressed as a structured checklist and scoring algorithm:

```text
# Roadmap Health Assessment (Table 11-1)
# Score each item: 0 = entirely/mostly no, 1 = sort of/maybe, 2 = definitely yes
# Maximum possible score: 22 points

ADD points for:
  [ ] Clear product vision stakeholders can explain               (0–2)
  [ ] Measurable business objectives stakeholders are aware of    (0–2)
  [ ] Roadmap focuses on customer needs                           (0–2)
  [ ] All items tied to customer needs or business objectives     (0–2)
  [ ] Roadmap updated regularly                                   (0–2)
  [ ] Time allowed to learn before committing to solutions        (0–2)
  [ ] Objective and accepted prioritization method in use         (0–2)
  [ ] Established alignment process with stakeholders             (0–2)
  [ ] Regularly present roadmap to key stakeholders               (0–2)
  [ ] Regularly present roadmap to customers                      (0–2)
  [ ] Seek and incorporate customer feedback                      (0–2)

SUBTRACT points for:
  [ ] Specific features/solutions/fixes/deliverables on roadmap   (0–2)
  [ ] Precise or best-case dates on roadmap                       (0–2)
  [ ] Solutions thoroughly designed before need placed on roadmap (0–2)
  [ ] Project info (resources/milestones/dependencies) in roadmap (0–2)

TOTAL SCORE: _____ / 22

INTERPRET:
  18–22  → Approach A: enhance a strong process
  12–17  → Approach A: significant improvements needed
  ≤ 11   → Approach B: full relaunch from scratch
```

Six-step relaunch decision logic expressed as pseudo-code:

```text
# Six-Step Roadmap Relaunch

step_1: run_health_assessment()
  → score = sum(add_items) - sum(subtract_items)

step_2: get_buyin(method: shuttle_diplomacy | co_creation_workshop)
  → goal: alignment on necessity for change (not full consensus)

step_3: train_stakeholders()
  → explain: no feature commitments, no date commitments
  → define: each stakeholder's role and obligation

step_4: if score >= 12
    improve_one_part_at_a_time(timeframe: "a few weeks")
  else  # score <= 11
    run_roadmap_workshop(duration: "a few days")
    limit_initial_circle_to: [product, engineering, design]

step_5: every 3–6 weeks
  steering_committee.review_progress()
  → apply discovery_period to process changes
  → decide: stay_course | change_course | new_direction

step_6: treat_every_launch_as_precursor_to_next_relaunch()
  # Roadmapping is continuous; resist treating it as a one-time fix
```
